Part I · Student Profile

Hello, I'm James.

I'm an Information Technology student at the University of Nueva Caceres in Naga City, and I come from Calabanga, Camarines Sur. Most of my days are spent writing code, mostly C# and .NET, designing databases, and fixing bugs that only appear at two in the morning.

That makes me an unusual reader of Great Books. Before this course, most of what I read was technical documentation, programming forums, and the occasional story I found online when I needed a break from a screen full of errors. I was used to reading for answers: what does this function return, why does this query fail? Literature asked me to read for something else entirely: questions, ambiguity, and meaning.

Reading & Me

Software developmentStories with moral weightEpics & heroesPuzzles & problem-solvingBicol rain & quiet nights

I've always liked stories with a clear conflict and a lesson inside them. As a kid I enjoyed fables and Bible stories, and later the hero stories I found in games and movies. What I didn't realize until this course is that many of those modern stories borrow their bones from the works we studied: the angry hero, the clever animal, the man who suffers without knowing why. Seeing those patterns trace back thousands of years made reading feel less like homework and more like discovering the source code of storytelling.

My Expectations & Experience in Great Books

Honestly, I expected Great Books to be a subject of long, old, difficult texts that had little to do with an IT student's life. I expected to memorize plots and authors. Instead, I found a course that trained the same skill I use every day in programming: looking at something complex, breaking it into parts, and understanding how those parts work together. The difference is that in literature the "system" is a human being, a society, or a belief.

I came in expecting to read old books. I'm leaving having learned to read people, power, and pain a little more carefully.

Part III · My Favorite Literary Masterpiece
The Book of Job · Chapter 38

Voice Out of the Whirlwind

Author unknown · Hebrew Bible, Wisdom Literature (c. 6th–4th century BCE)

A Brief Overview

The Book of Job tells the story of a man who is "perfect and upright" in every way. He is wealthy, devoted to God, and loved by his family. In a scene set in heaven, the Adversary (Satan) argues that Job is only faithful because he is blessed. God allows Job to be tested. In a single day Job loses his livestock, his servants and all ten of his children. Then he is struck with painful sores from head to foot.

  1. The TestJob loses everything but refuses to curse God: "the Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away."
  2. The FriendsEliphaz, Bildad and Zophar insist Job must have sinned, because in their view suffering is always punishment.
  3. The ProtestJob defends his innocence and demands that God answer him face to face.
  4. The WhirlwindIn chapter 38, God finally speaks out of a storm, not with explanations but with a flood of questions about creation.
  5. The RestorationJob humbles himself. God rebukes the friends for speaking wrongly and restores Job's fortunes.

Why It Is My Favorite

I chose this text because it does something I did not expect from scripture: it lets a good man argue with God, and it does not pretend his pain is simple. Job's friends have a neat formula, if you suffer, you sinned, and the book shows that formula is wrong. As someone who works with logic every day, I was struck that the book refuses to reduce human suffering to an if-then statement.

What moved me most is the answer Job receives. He wants a reason, and God gives him a universe instead: the foundations of the earth, the boundaries of the sea, the storehouses of snow, the wild goat giving birth where no human sees. The message is not "be quiet." It is closer to "your view is too small for the whole picture, but you are not forgotten inside it." Job never learns about the wager in heaven. He is not given the explanation. He is given an encounter, and that turns out to be enough.

Themes, Characters & Lessons

The Limits of Human Understanding

God's questions ("Hast thou entered into the springs of the sea?") remind Job, and me, how much of reality runs beyond our knowledge and control. Humility here is not humiliation. It is seeing ourselves accurately.

Suffering Is Not Always Punishment

The friends confuse correlation with cause. God's final rebuke shows that blaming victims for their pain is itself a moral failure.

Job: Honest Faith

Job complains, questions and grieves, yet never walks away. His faith is not silent obedience. It is a relationship strong enough to hold an argument.

The Friends: Easy Answers

They show up and sit with Job for seven days, which is good. Then they start explaining his pain, which is not. Sometimes presence helps more than answers.

Personal Connections & Realizations

Here in Camarines Sur we know storms. Every typhoon season, and especially after the severe flooding that hit Naga and our province during Kristine in 2024, I have heard people ask Job's question: why us, why now, what did we do? I have also heard Job's friends: people who say a disaster is a punishment, or that those who lost the most must have deserved it. Reading Job 38 changed how I hear both voices. It is fine to ask why. It is not fine to answer someone else's suffering with blame.

The text also helped me as a student. There are semesters when I put in the work and things still go wrong: a project crashes before a deadline, a plan falls apart. Job taught me that not having the explanation is not the same as being abandoned. Sometimes the right response is the one Job finally gives: to step back, see that the picture is bigger than my corner of it, and keep going.

Job did not get an answer. He got a bigger view. That is the gift I want to keep from this book.

Part IV · Summary of Learning

What Great Books Taught Me

This course took me across almost three thousand years of storytelling. Here is the knowledge, the lenses and the skills I am taking with me.

1 · Masterpieces Across Time & Cultures

I learned that great literature is not just Western or just "old." The works we read came from different civilizations, and each reflects the beliefs and anxieties of the society that produced it. The Hebrew wisdom tradition wrestles with God and suffering. The Indian tradition teaches statecraft through animal fables. The Greek epic celebrates glory while mourning its cost. A woman of the Japanese court quietly records the emotional lives of people around her. Novels from Europe and America confront class, injustice and race. Placing them on one timeline showed me that people everywhere have asked the same big questions, only in different languages and forms.

2 · Literary Lenses I Learned to Apply

The most practical tool I gained is the idea of a literary lens: a deliberate angle for reading a text. One story can produce completely different insights depending on the questions you bring to it. These are the lenses I used across my seven analyses:

Used on Job 38Moralist–Philosophical

Asks what a work teaches about right living, truth and the human condition.

Used on The PanchatantraFormalist

Focuses on the text itself: structure, form, imagery and how they create meaning.

Used on The IliadArchetypal

Finds universal patterns, such as the hero, the fatal flaw and the journey, shared across cultures.

Used on The Tale of GenjiFeminist

Examines how women are represented, silenced or empowered within a text and its society.

Used on Pride and PrejudiceMarxist

Reads a work through class, money, property and economic power.

Used on Les MisérablesPsychoanalytic

Explores the unconscious, inner conflict, and the id, ego and superego in characters.

Used on To Kill a MockingbirdHistorical–Sociological

Connects a text to the real time, place and social conditions that shaped it.

3 · Literature as a Mirror of Human Experience

I now see literature as a record of what it has meant to be human in different times and places. Achilles' grief over Patroclus, Elizabeth Bennet's refusal to marry for convenience, Valjean's guilt, Scout's loss of innocence: none of these feel distant once you look past the setting. Literature preserves the beliefs, injustices and hopes of societies, so reading it is a way of hearing voices I would never otherwise meet.

Part V · Personal Reflection

Looking Back on the Journey

Five questions, answered as honestly as I can.

Question 01

How has my understanding and appreciation of literature changed?

At the start of the semester I thought of literature as a collection of old stories to summarize and memorize. I read for plot: who did what, and what happened at the end. Now I understand that the plot is only the surface. Underneath are choices the author made about structure, symbols, who gets to speak and who stays silent. I learned to appreciate that a story written three thousand years ago in Greece, or a thousand years ago in Japan, can still describe exactly what I feel. My appreciation grew from "this is required" to "this is actually about me, too."

Question 02

What challenges did I encounter, and how did I overcome them?

The biggest challenge was language and length. Translations of the Iliad and Job use old, formal English, and works like Les Misérables and The Tale of Genji are enormous, with many characters and long digressions. I also struggled at first to tell the difference between summarizing a story and analyzing it.

I overcame this the way I approach a large codebase: I broke it into modules. I read summaries and background first to get the overall structure, then focused on key chapters and passages. I kept notes on characters and themes, and I chose one lens before writing so my analysis had a clear direction. Class discussions also helped a lot. Hearing my classmates' interpretations showed me details I had missed.

Question 03

Which literary lens influenced my interpretation the most, and why?

The Moralist–Philosophical lens influenced me the most, especially in my reading of Job. It pushed me past "what happens" to "what does this say about how we should live?" Even when I used other lenses, I kept coming back to that question. I was also surprised by the Marxist lens. Reading Pride and Prejudice as a story about money and property, not just romance, showed me how much economic pressure shapes people's choices, then and now.

Question 04

What realizations about life, society, culture and human nature did I gain?

I realized that human nature has barely changed. Pride, jealousy, grief, the hunger for respect and the fear of suffering appear in every work, from Achilles to Javert. I also realized that societies often build systems that hurt the people with the least power: women in the Heian court, the poor in Hugo's France, Black Americans in Lee's Maycomb. Literature lets us see those systems from the inside. Finally, I learned from Job and from Valjean that mercy and humility are often stronger than being right.

Question 05

How can I apply these lessons in my personal, academic and professional life?

Personally, I want to be less like Job's friends: slower to judge people who are struggling and quicker to simply be present. Academically, the habit of reading closely, supporting claims with evidence and considering multiple perspectives will help me in research, documentation and our capstone work. Professionally, as a future IT professional I will be building systems that real people use. Les Misérables and To Kill a Mockingbird reminded me that rules and systems can be unjust if they ignore the human beings inside them. I want to build software with that awareness: fair, accessible and designed with empathy for the user who is not like me.

Great Books did not turn me from an IT student into a literature major. It made me an IT student who reads better, thinks more carefully, and remembers that behind every system is a story about people.
— James Manuel An
